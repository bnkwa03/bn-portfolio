Stanford · Design for Behavior Change

Sage

Behavioral research that turned an AI financial coach from a spending-restriction tool into one built around awareness and intention.

Bennie NkwantabisaUX ResearcherTeam of 4

Role
UX Researcher, team of 4
Context
Stanford, Design for Behavior Change
Focus
Generative behavioral research
Methods
Diary study, assumption test, AI field study, usability

The problem

Budgeting tools tend to be reactive or effortful. They record spending after it happens, or they ask for sustained manual tracking. The team’s initial product carried a stronger assumption, that users wanted their impulse spending stopped, and it was built to restrict that behavior.

Mapped against existing tools on two axes, timing and effort, that assumption left a clear gap and a clear risk. The research set out to test whether restriction was the right premise at all.

Competitive positioning across timing of intervention and effort required.

Methodology

Each study tested the restriction assumption from a different angle. Expand a study to see why the method fit and what it revealed.

Why this method

In-the-moment logging captures frequent convenience purchases without the distortion of recall.

What it revealed

Whether spending felt problematic depended on identity and values more than on impulsivity.

Why this method

The restriction premise was the highest-risk assumption in the product.

What it revealed

Eight of fourteen wanted to reduce impulse spending, and only five strongly.

Why this method

To test whether a reflective, non-punitive coach changed awareness or behavior.

What it revealed

Awareness changed faster than behavior, and morning intention-setting created more friction than evening reflection.

Why this method

To check whether the core loop was legible before further build.

What it revealed

The AI coach did not feel central, and the loop was not legible.

Impact & highlights

The evidence reframed Sage from a tool that restricts spending into one that supports awareness and intention.

Evidence

Not all participants saw impulse spending as undesirable.

Product response

Sage helps users reduce unplanned spending without blocking purchases or imposing budget limits.

Evidence

Morning planning introduced useful friction before some purchases occurred.

Product response

Daily intention-setting became central to the experience rather than a setup step.

Evidence

Some users valued deep probing while others found persistent questioning exhausting.

Product response

AI coaching adjusts depth and tone rather than applying one reflection pattern to every purchase.

Evidence

Awareness rose more consistently across the study than purchase reduction did.

Product response

Success is measured by awareness and intention, not by short-term spending cuts alone.

Where the AI coach lives

The dashboard treated the AI as one destination among several, and logging a purchase redirected users into a chat they had not asked for. Some read it as customer support and avoided it.

The AI coach becomes the primary landing experience, introduced during onboarding, and prompts users to state the day’s planned purchases, which makes plan, spend, reflect the spine of the product.

The studies were small, self-reported, and five days long, so they establish awareness and intention as the mechanisms to test next, not sustained behavior change.
