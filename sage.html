01Problem

Impulsive spenders needed an effective way to manage spending.

Current financial management platforms focus on budgeting or expense tracking, not the emotional and contextual factors that drive impulse spending.

02Methodology

Literature Review

Main insights
  • Environmental Triggers: Impulse spending is influenced by environmental cues (e.g., product proximity, sales notifications, or social media exposure, emotional cues (e.g., feelings of FOMO, boredom, and negative mood), psychological needs, and social needs (Thorndike, 1937; Wood & Neal, 2009; Rodrigues et al., 2021; Jafri et al., 2025; Nguyen, 2025)
  • Designed Spending Environments: Retail ecosystems are intentionally designed to encourage impulse purchases by reducing friction through features like discounts, social influence, and intentional refund policies. (Moser et al., 2019)
  • Effective Interventions: Research suggests a number of intervention approaches to curtailing impulsive spending behavior. This included proactively strategizing, encouraging reflection, imposing spending limits, and enforcing delays before purchase. (Moser et al. 2019; Peetz & Davydenko, 2021; Davydenko & Peetz, 2024)
  • Personalization: Identifying a user's emotional needs is critical for creating reflective interventions that address the root cause. (Thorndike, 1937; Peetz & Davydenko, 2021)

Comparative Analysis

Key insights
  • Preventative tools, such as structured budgeting systems, often require high user effort, which can limit sustained use.
  • Several applications rely on cashback or reward incentives, which may unintentionally encourage spending rather than reduce it.
  • Most applications tend to focus on the amount spent, but not what is being purchased and why
  • Most applications did not challenge justifications made in the moment
OpportunityThere's a gap in the market for a solution that combines quantitative data on spending with qualitative insights on how a user feels in the moment of a purchase.

Baseline Study

Pre/post-study interview, diary study

9participants
5days logging
What I did

Ran research ops: wrote the study outline and screener questions.

Ran a 5-day diary study with 9 convenience spenders, logging a short questionnaire after each purchase.

Synthesized with thematic analysis across pre, during, and post interviews.

Justification

In-the-moment logging plus pre and post interviews captured both the numbers (frequency, cost, impulsivity, guilt) and the why (motivation, emotion, context), for a fuller read on what people treat as planned or unplanned.

Key insights
01Behavior

Guilt-driven reframing

Participants quickly recast unplanned buys as "necessary," "productive," or "goal-aligned" to sidestep guilt.

02Awareness

Hidden totals

People underestimated how much they spent until they logged it.

03Values

Identity over impulsivity

Whether a purchase feels problematic depends more on how it fits someone's identity and values than on cost or frequency. Constraints can turn a want into a "non-negotiable need" and move the guilt onto the circumstances.

What I learned

Sage has to surface how often impulse purchases really happen, and the justifications behind them, so people cannot lean on physical, social, temporal, or situational context to explain them away.

Intervention Study

Pre/post-study interview, diary study with AI reflection agent I created

7participants (1 returning)
5days
Research goal

Test whether daily intention-setting plus AI-guided reflection raises awareness of impulse triggers and reduces rapid post-purchase justification.

What I did

Built a reflective AI agent (custom GPT) to probe the meaning behind unplanned purchases.

Participants set morning intentions, then reflected with the agent each evening on anything unplanned.

Key insights
01Success metric

Awareness over dollars

Success should mean consistent awareness of impulses, not just spending less.

02Timing

Morning beats evening

Setting intentions in the morning did more to curb small, in-the-moment buys than evening reflection.

Intention-setting became a central design pillar, not one of two equal steps.

03Coach tone

Tone shapes trust

AI tone and persistence drove engagement. Too pushy read as shaming or manipulative.

The coach adapts its depth to the stakes, with guardrails against shame or over-validation.

Assumption Test

14respondents
5strong desire to stop

Rapid testing to support the primary assumption surrounding our platform's main intervention mechanism:

Impulse spenders want to stop spending impulsively.
Result

Only 5 of 14 self-identified impulse spenders expressed a strong desire to stop spending impulsively. Though n=14 is a small sample size, the results should have provided a strong enough signal to show that the majority in this group would reaffirm our assumption, even in a small representation.

Usability Testing

Ran research ops, established usability study tasks and study guide for administrator.

Major insight / change

AI logging/coach was not clearly established as the primary functionality or central interaction point based on the visual design.Positioned AI coach as landing page to establish order of actions for users (clarified visual hierarchy to align user journey with primary functionality).

Coach read as one destination among several→ Coach becomes the landing page
Order of actions unclear→ Morning logging becomes the first action
Primary functionality not legible→ Visual hierarchy aligned to the user journey
03Biggest impact from insight

Interface design repositioned the app's AI financial coach to be the primary screen so that morning logging of planned purchases was intuitively the first action from a user.

This project wrapped up after the first round of usability testing, so the impact of our major change was not measured.
