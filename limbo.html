Rebuilding the Intimacy of Proximity for Friendships That Lost It

A survey on private social spaces, a forced-choice prompt ranking, and a 7-day beta diary study shaped Limbo's prompt strategy and six shipped product decisions. The study that mattered most was the one that failed.

Executive Summary

New grads lose the forced proximity of college, and with it the low-stakes daily contact that kept friendships close. Friendships stall in what participants called a "limbo zone" — too close to drop, not close enough to maintain without effort.

Limbo is a daily prompt app built to manufacture the randomness proximity used to supply for free. As the only researcher on a three-person team shipping under a fixed timeline, every study had to earn its place against build time.

The most useful thing I found was that our core mechanic did not work. Engagement in the beta collapsed within 48 hours — but testers kept thinking about the prompts and discussing them with friends off-platform. That divergence between a working prompt and a failing container drove the redesign that followed.

01 — Framing the Problem

How do new grads cultivate relationships after losing the comfort of forced proximity?

Needfinding ran as a team effort — 7 in-depth interviews and 8 quick chats with new grads 0–3 years out. I ran a share of the interviews and owned the study scaffolding: assumptions, unknowns, interview goals, and the synthesis into findings and needs.

New grads describe college friendships in childhood language.

The intimacy came from exposure, not effort. Nobody scheduled it.

Calls are reserved for when there's an "accumulated list."

Synchronous contact had become a special event requiring justification.

"Only if we are separated for long periods of time and we have, like, accumulated a list of things we want to catch up about." — Jared, new grad

New grads distinguish "discovery" platforms from "cultivation" tools.

They reject discovery outright when their existing network feels good enough — which ruled out an entire product direction before we built anything.

That left a question I didn't want to answer by assumption: intimacy survives distance in some digital spaces and not others, so what makes the difference? I surveyed 23 people about the workaround they'd already built for themselves — close friends stories, finstas, private stories — using matched question blocks so each respondent rated their private space and their main account against the same battery.

5.1 / 7

felt intimacy of private spaces

vs. 3.0 / 7 for the same respondents' main accounts · n = 23

★ Key Insight

Intimacy did not come from a private space existing. It came from a private space existing inside a larger public one.

The contrast is the mechanism — main accounts drew words like milestone and performative, while private spaces were explicitly for the things that weren't worth posting.

This is why Limbo was built as a small private core inside a larger ecosystem rather than a walled garden.

02 — What Makes a Good Prompt

Revelation 1: What people love discussing is not what they want to answer.

Study Design

I built a pairwise forced-choice sorter — the "bias sort" method people use to rank album tracks. Participants chose between two prompts at a time against two stated criteria: how much they wanted to answer it, and how much they wanted to see their friends' answers. Repeated comparisons resolved into a full 1–12 ranking, output as an encoded result code participants pasted into a follow-up survey, which let me attach each ranking to qualitative reasoning from the same person.

Rating scales compress — everything lands on a 4. Forced trade-offs surface actual preference order, and that's precisely where this paid off.

n = 22 · 11 in the 18–25 target segment · 12 prompts · 4 categories

CategoryExampleAll (n = 22)Target 18–25 (n = 11)
Personal"What random hobby have you always thought about trying?"5.275.39
Hypothetical"If you woke up in someone else's body…"6.385.85
Opinion"Using only the top 5 songs from any artist, who's best?"7.327.55
Debate"Is a hot dog more of a taco or a sandwich?"7.918.14

Average rank by prompt category (1 = most preferred of 12). Lower is better.

★ Key Insight

This contradicted our own pilot.

Both pilot participants had talked at length, and with obvious enjoyment, about the debate prompts — so the team's read was that debate bait drove conversation. Ranked against each other under stated criteria, debate came dead last.

Conversation volume is not the same as desire to answer. I only caught the difference because the method forced trade-offs instead of collecting ratings.

The virtual factor overrides trust.

Participants hesitated to post revealing answers regardless of how close the audience was.

"A screenshot goes a long way." — Josh, concept pilot

That killed the assumption that a trusted audience is enough to unlock candor.

"Conversation starter" carries stigma among close friends.

The framing implies stranger-management. Participants wanted new things to discuss but bristled at being asked something a stranger would ask.

That second finding set the bar for the question bank I wrote: a prompt passes if a close friend asking it reads as "they want to know me better" rather than "why are you talking to me like a stranger?" It's why "What's your most irrational ick?" stayed in and "dinner with anyone, dead or alive" did not.

What shipped: prompt sequencing built around a low-floor, high-ceiling opener set — personal, fast to answer, high variance in others' answers — with shock-factor prompts staged later, once buy-in existed.

03 — The Beta That Failed

Revelation 2: The prompts worked. The container didn't.

Study Design

We had a working build. Concept testing said people liked the idea; I needed to know whether they liked the execution, and specifically whether they'd still be there on day 8. Seven-day retention was the metric I set, because a product premised on low effort has to survive the moment novelty wears off.

I built the pilot system end to end: onboarding capturing how each person intended to test, a daily entry form due before the next 4pm prompt drop, a separate bug and feedback channel, a participant resource doc, and raffle-based compensation. I wrote the first 30 days of prompts and the daily instrument — which prompt, how excited to answer, how excited to see others' answers, and I like / I wish / I wonder.

37 signed up · 20 onboarded with a stated testing plan · 7-day waves

  • Day 1: strong. Accounts created, first prompt answered.
  • Day 2: steep decline.
  • Day 3: effectively zero.
  • No participant completed a full 7-day wave.

And yet

testers reported thinking about the prompt later in the day and talking about it with friends off-platform.

★ Key Insight

The prompts were doing their job. The container wasn't.

A working mechanic inside a failing product is a different problem than a bad mechanic, and it pointed the redesign somewhere specific.

Diagnosis — three causes

Cold start.

With few friends on the platform, answering produced nothing back. The reward for showing up was entirely contingent on other people showing up.

No standalone benefit.

If you weren't using Circles, there was nothing to do once you'd answered. A user who arrived alone hit a dead end in under a minute.

A platform constraint I'd flagged going in.

This wave ran on our web app, which had no push notifications — a retention risk I identified before the study and raised with our coach. I designed manual reminder messaging into the protocol to partially compensate, but it can't substitute for native notifications, so I treated the number as directional and set TestFlight approval as the gate for a clean retention read.

04 — Research → What Shipped

★ Principle

When designing for one-on-one, design for intimacy.
When designing for groups, design for play.

Cross-reading the survey against the ranking study produced the principle that governed the next build. Rather than translating findings into isolated features, I used them to define how the product should behave — then derived the features from that.

Research FindingProduct Decision
Depth lives in one-on-one; public comments make it "everyone's business"Comments route to a DM, not a public thread — the reaction becomes a conversation
Intimacy requires a larger ecosystem to contrast againstExplore layer: a low-effort daily opinion slider and opt-in public feed, expanding the ecosystem without diluting the private core
Cold start left solo users with nothing to doScrapbook profile and Lightning Round quick-fire prompts — standalone value that still reveals personality to close friends
Groups want play; pairs want depthGame and event modes for circles, with circle-specific prompts
Buy-in depends on everyone answering the same thingFeed lock — you can't see anyone's answers until you post your own
Prompts drove off-platform conversation even when retention failedShareable prompt cards, so the conversation a prompt starts can travel

That last row also drove the launch. If the prompt is what travels, put the prompt in the world and let the platform follow: 24 poster prompts selected by ranked performance, installed overnight around graduate residences and senior friend groups — the two populations needfinding pointed at. Scanning the QR let people answer and see others' answers without installing anything, so they could experience the core loop before committing to it.

05 — What I Know, and What I Don't

What the research established

Intimacy in digital spaces depends on contrast — a private room inside a public building, not a private building. Personal prompts outperform debate, opinion, and hypothetical prompts on both willingness to answer and interest in others' answers, consistently across age segments. And platform visibility suppresses candor independently of how much a user trusts the audience.

What it did not establish

Samples were small and drawn from convenience channels, skewed toward women — 18 of 22 in the ranking study — and toward college students. The ranking finding is directional, not representative. The beta ran without push notifications, so it isn't a clean test of the mechanic, and seven days was too short a window to answer the question I cared about, which was day-8 return.

What I'd do differently

Gate the retention study on notifications existing, or run a matched arm with manual daily SMS to isolate the variable. Extend to a 14-day wave with a 30-day passive follow-up.

And replace the daily diary form. A daily form due before a moving deadline was too heavy an instrument for a product whose entire premise is low effort — the study's compliance problem was a version of the product's retention problem, and I should have seen that coming. Behavioral instrumentation would have told me on day two what the diary took a week to not tell me.

In Closing

Limbo shipped to the App Store as a daily prompt platform built on a small private core inside a larger public ecosystem — one-on-one depth in the center, play at the edges, and a question bank engineered so that being asked feels like being known rather than being screened.

When the evidence contradicted what the team believed — about debate prompts, about our own retention hopes — my job was to say so early enough that it changed the build instead of the postmortem.
