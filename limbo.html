Limbo: Keeping Post-Grad Friendships Active with Daily Prompts

How testing an iOS alpha build led us to pivot from solo user sign-ups to group cohort onboarding.

Overview

After graduating college, friends scatter across different cities and lose the daily, spontaneous interactions that kept them close. Reaching out often degrades into occasional, high-effort catch-up calls or silent social media scrolling.

Limbo is a mobile app that sends a daily prompt to small, private friend groups. It gives friends a quick, low-pressure excuse to interact every day through text, photo responses, drawings, and binary sliders.

My Role & Impact

01

End-to-End Research

Sole researcher across 11 initial user interviews, a 2-part prompt sorting study (18 participants for prompt ranking, 23 for privacy norms), and a live TestFlight test with 20 users.

02

Identifying the Drop-off

Discovered through TestFlight app data and exit interviews that users who signed up alone stopped using the app after 1–2 days because their friends weren’t there to see or answer prompts.

03

Changing Onboarding

Showed the team that people are motivated by seeing their friends’ answers more than just posting their own. Rebuilt onboarding so users joined together in friend groups (onboarding 37 beta testers in cohorts).

01 — Problem

Why Post-Grad Friendships Drift

When forced proximity disappears, reaching out requires effort. Through initial interviews, three main friction points emerged:

01

Lack of Structure

People want to stay in touch, but nobody knows what to send on a random Tuesday.

02

High Activation Barrier

Catching up feels like it requires a 1-hour phone call or major life update.

03

Fear of Being Overbearing

Users worry about bothering friends who seem busy.

02 — Research Plan

How I Tested the Product

I ran research across three main stages to test our assumptions from initial idea to live app:

01

User Interviews (n = 11)

Spoke with recent grads to understand how their communication habits changed after college and where friendships fell off.

02

Prompt Ranking & Privacy Survey (n = 18 & n = 23)

Built an interactive sorting tool in Claude so 18 participants could rank different prompt topics by how much they liked them and how comfortable they felt answering them.

Surveyed 23 people on how they felt about sharing private moments in small groups versus public social feeds.

03

Live Alpha Test on TestFlight (n = 20)

Put an early iOS build in front of 20 users to track daily app usage and interviewed them after a week to see why they stayed or left.

03 — Findings & Changes

Finding 1: Catch-Up Calls Feel Too Heavy

What I Found: Participants avoided starting conversations because they felt a text or call had to be about big updates like jobs or moving.

“When you don’t see a person that often, you feel like the time you have together just has to be spent catching up.” — Skye, Study Participant

What We Did: Built asynchronous Daily Prompts that expire after 24 hours, giving friends an easy, low-stakes reason to send a quick reply without needing a big update.

Finding 2: Generic Prompts Bore People; Overly Personal Ones Stress People Out

What I Found: In the prompt ranking study (n = 18, ranked 1 to 10 where 1 = Favorite), generic icebreakers were rated as boring, while deep personal questions felt uncomfortable without existing trust. The best-received prompts were creative, playful scenarios.

Top-Ranked Prompt Examples:

  1. “What random hobby have you always thought about trying?” (Average Rank: 4.30)
  2. “If you woke up in someone else’s body, how would you convince your loved ones?” (Average Rank: 4.40)
  3. “What are you still lowkey scared of?” (Average Rank: 5.30)

What We Did: Grouped prompts by topic depth (light/funny vs. personal) and added different response styles like drawing canvases and opinion sliders alongside text.

Finding 3: Signing Up Alone Killed App Retention

What I Found: During the 20-person TestFlight alpha, 55% of users answered at least one prompt, but 0% kept using the app for a full week. Exit interviews showed that users who joined alone had no friends on their feed to read or respond to. Seeing friends’ answers (5.1/10 rating) was a much bigger reason to open the app than just posting an answer alone (4.3/10).

What We Did: Convinced the team to change onboarding from individual sign-ups to group invites, requiring users to join with 3–15 friends. This brought in 37 beta testers onboarded across friend cohorts.

04 — What We Built

Key Features in the Final Test Build

01

Multiple Response Types

Users can reply to daily prompts using text, drawing canvases, photo uploads, or quick slider votes.

02

Response History Grid

Answers build up into a private feed for the group over time.

03

Status Indicators

Simple profile tags show when a friend hasn’t checked in recently, making it easy to send a nudge.

Reflection & Limitations

What I Learned

1 · Research the social network, not just the interface.

Testing a social app with individual users doesn’t work. The experience depends entirely on whether a user’s real friends are active on the platform.

2 · Be honest about early data.

Identifying the 0% retention rate on the initial build gave us the proof we needed to redesign onboarding before launch.

Project Limitations

Small Alpha Group (n = 20)

While 20 users gave us clear feedback on why people dropped off, it is a small sample for drawing firm quantitative conclusions.

Post-Pivot Tracking Scope

We onboarded 37 beta testers into group cohorts, but we were not able to track 30-day retention for those groups before the 10-week capstone ended.
