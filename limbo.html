Rebuilding the Intimacy of Proximity for Friendships That Lost It

Four studies across ~80 participants — a survey on private social spaces, a forced-choice prompt ranking, two concept pilots, and a 7-day beta diary study — shaped Limbo’s prompt strategy, four shipped features, and its go-to-market campaign. The study that mattered most was the one that failed.

Executive Summary

New grads lose the forced proximity of college, and with it the low-stakes daily contact that kept friendships close. Calls start requiring a reason. Friendships stall in what our participants called a “limbo zone” — too close to drop, not close enough to maintain without effort.

Limbo is a daily prompt app built to manufacture the randomness proximity used to supply for free. I was the only researcher on a three-person team shipping under a fixed timeline, which meant every study had to earn its place against build time.

Across four studies I established what kind of question people actually want to answer, why intimacy survives in some digital spaces and not others, and — most usefully — that our core mechanic did not work. The beta failed: engagement collapsed within 48 hours. But testers kept thinking about the prompts and kept discussing them with friends off-platform. That divergence between a working prompt and a failing container became the most valuable finding in the project, and it drove the redesign that followed.

01 — Framing the Problem

How do new grads cultivate relationships after losing the comfort of forced proximity provided by college?

Needfinding ran as a team effort: 7 in-depth interviews and 8 quick chats with new grads 0–3 years out, scattered across cities. I ran a share of the interviews — including new grads from Stanford and SDSU — and owned the study scaffolding: the initial assumptions, unknowns, interview goals, and the synthesis into findings, needs, and How Might We statements.

Finding 01: New grads describe college friendships in childhood language.

The intimacy came from exposure, not effort. Nobody scheduled it.

Finding 02: Calls are reserved for when there’s an “accumulated list.”

Synchronous contact had become a special event requiring justification.

“Only if we are separated for long periods of time and we have, like, accumulated a list of things we want to catch up about.” — Jared, new grad

Finding 03: New grads distinguish “discovery” platforms from “cultivation” tools.

They reject discovery outright when their existing network feels good enough. This ruled out an entire product direction before we built anything.

Finding 04: Casual connections stall in a “limbo zone.”

Participants struggled to move situational connections into deeper ones without a pretext.

Finding 05: Virtual relationship dynamics get misread.

Participants misjudged where they stood with casual connections, wasting effort or losing friendships they wanted to keep.

★ Key Conceptual Insight

The product could not be about meeting people. It had to be about maintaining them — and it had to manufacture the randomness that proximity used to supply for free.

How Might We

  • …use shared experiences as an anchor for close connections?
  • …add randomness into a long-lasting relationship?
  • …make relationship effort feel mutual and welcomed rather than awkward?

02 — How Intimacy Survives Online

Revelation 1: Intimacy doesn’t come from privacy. It comes from privacy inside something public.

Why I ran it

Needfinding told us intimacy survived distance in some digital spaces but not others. Rather than guess at what made those spaces work, I studied the workaround people had already built for themselves: close friends stories, finstas, spam accounts, private stories.

Study Design

46-item survey with matched blocks — participants answered the same battery about their private space and their main space, enabling a within-subject comparison rather than relying on recall. Distributed across Reddit, community mailing lists, and survey-exchange channels to reach beyond the Stanford population.

n = 23 · Ages 18–29 · Primarily college students · Skewed women

5.1 / 7

felt intimacy of private spaces

vs. 3.0 / 7 for the same respondents’ main accounts

Finding 01: Private spaces fill the gap between a DM and a broadcast.

Nearly all held under 100 people. Participants wanted more engagement than a text affords, but less exposure than a post demands. There was no product occupying that middle.

Finding 02: The vocabulary splits cleanly.

Main accounts drew words like milestone, performative, curated — “what you want people to think about you.” Private spaces were explicitly for the things that weren’t worth posting.

Finding 03: Inclusion is a two-way trust signal, and it obligates reciprocity.

Being added means “I trust you.” Exclusion isn’t read as rejection — it’s read as a measure of proximity. One respondent is out as gay only in their close space; others use theirs for controversial opinions or being “real and raw” without judgment.

★ Key Conceptual Insight

Intimacy did not come from a private space existing. It came from a private space existing inside a larger public one. The contrast is the mechanism.

This is why Limbo was built as a small private core inside a larger ecosystem rather than as a walled garden — and it later gave me the evidence to argue for a public Explore layer without diluting what made the core work.

03 — What Makes a Good Prompt

Revelation 2: What people love discussing is not what they want to answer.

Why I ran it

We knew the product was a daily question game. We did not know what a good question was — and my read of pilot behavior did not match what participants said in conversation.

Study Design

I built a pairwise forced-choice sorter — the “bias sort” method people use to rank album tracks — and deployed it as a standalone tool. Participants saw two prompts at a time and chose between them against two stated criteria: how much they wanted to answer it, and how much they wanted to see their friends’ answers. Repeated comparisons resolved into a full 1–12 ranking, output as an encoded result code participants pasted into a follow-up survey. That pairing let me attach each quantitative ranking to qualitative reasoning from the same person.

Recruited through the participant pool I’d built, plus Stanford mailing lists, group chats, and Nextdoor. Compensated via raffle entry.

n = 22 completed rankings · 11 in the 18–25 target segment · 12 prompts · 4 categories

Why forced choice: Rating scales compress — everything lands on a 4. Trade-offs surface actual preference order. This is precisely where it paid off.

Personal prompts won in both segments; debate prompts finished last in both. Highest-ranked individual prompt: “What random hobby have you always thought about trying? Why haven’t you tried yet?” (3.82). Lowest: a would-you-rather (8.77).

★ Key Conceptual Insight

This contradicted our own pilot. Both pilot participants had talked at length, and with obvious enjoyment, about the debate prompts — so the team’s read was that debate bait drove conversation. Ranked against each other under stated criteria, debate came dead last.

Conversation volume is not the same as desire to answer. I only caught the difference because the method forced trade-offs instead of collecting ratings.

Findings from the paired qualitative

Finding 01: Novelty isn’t required. Interest is.

Prompts participants had heard and discussed many times still ranked high. We had assumed novelty was the constraint; it wasn’t.

Finding 02: The virtual factor overrides trust.

Participants hesitated to post revealing answers regardless of how close the audience was.

“A screenshot goes a long way.” — Josh, concept pilot

Perception is a constant consideration even inside a private space, which killed the assumption that a trusted audience is sufficient to unlock candor.

Finding 03: “Conversation starter” carries stigma among close friends.

The framing implies stranger-management. Participants wanted new things to discuss but bristled at being asked something a stranger would ask.

Product translation

That third finding set the bar for the ~200-prompt bank I wrote. A prompt passes if a close friend asking it reads as “they want to know me better” rather than “why are you talking to me like a stranger?” It’s why “What’s your most irrational ick?” stayed in and “dinner with anyone, dead or alive” did not.

What shipped: prompt sequencing built around a low-floor, high-ceiling opener set — personal, fast to answer, high variance in other people’s answers — with shock-factor prompts staged later, once buy-in existed. I also pushed to weight one-on-one friendships over friend groups here, since not every user has a group to keep warm; many have individual friends whose only common thread is them.

04 — The Beta That Failed

Revelation 3: The prompts worked. The container didn’t.

Why I ran it

We had a working build. Concept testing said people liked the idea. I needed to know whether they liked the execution — and specifically whether they would still be there on day 8. Seven-day retention was the metric I set, because a product premised on low effort has to survive the moment novelty wears off.

Study Design

I built the entire pilot system: an onboarding form capturing how each person intended to test (solo, with 1–2 friends, small group, large group), a daily entry form due before the next 4pm prompt drop, a separate bug and feedback channel, a participant resource document, and raffle-based compensation with additional entries for continued participation.

I wrote the first 30 days of prompts and the daily instrument: which prompt, how excited they were to answer, how excited they were to see others’ answers, and I like / I wish / I wonder.

37 signed up · 20 onboarded with a stated testing plan (7 in small groups, 5 with 1–2 friends, 5 solo, 3 in large groups) · 7-day waves

D1

Day 1: strong

Accounts created, first prompt answered, early enthusiasm.

D2

Day 2: steep decline

Engagement dropped sharply.

D3

Day 3: effectively zero

Activity flatlined.

No participant completed a full 7-day wave.

And yet

Testers reported thinking about the prompt later in the day and talking about it with friends off-platform.

★ Key Conceptual Insight

The prompts were doing their job. The container wasn’t. That divergence — a working mechanic inside a failing product — is the whole finding, and it’s what made the failure useful rather than just disappointing.

Diagnosis — three causes

Cause 01: Cold start.

With few friends on the platform, answering produced nothing back. The reward for showing up was entirely contingent on other people showing up.

Cause 02: No standalone benefit.

If you weren’t using Circles, there was nothing else to do once you’d answered. A user who arrived alone hit a dead end in under a minute.

Cause 03: A known platform constraint I’d flagged going in.

This wave ran on our web app, which had no push notifications — a constraint I identified as a retention risk before the study and raised with our coach. I designed manual reminder messaging into the protocol to partially compensate, but it can’t fully substitute for native notifications, so I treated the retention number as directional rather than clean and set TestFlight approval as the gate for a proper retention read.

Supporting finding — Circles

Circles came back mixed in pilot, and one participant articulated exactly why:

“Having multiple different questions makes everything more complicated… I will always have the most buy-in to the central question being asked every day. Knowing that everyone’s answering the same question is what makes me want to do it.” — beta participant

Segmentation was competing with the single mechanic that made the product feel like an event.

05 — Research → Product Decisions

Cross-reading the survey against the ranking study produced the principle that governed the next build.

★ The Principle

When designing for one-on-one, design for intimacy.
When designing for groups, design for play.

Rather than translating findings into isolated features, I used them to define how the product should behave — then derived the features from that.

What the Research Found → What Shipped

Research FindingProduct Decision
Depth lives in one-on-one; public comments make it “everyone’s business”Comments route to a DM, not a public thread — the reaction becomes a conversation
Intimacy requires a larger ecosystem to contrast againstExplore layer added: a low-effort daily opinion slider and an opt-in public feed, expanding the ecosystem without diluting the private core
Cold start left solo users with nothing to doScrapbook profile (six categories of micro-answers) and Lightning Round quick-fire prompts — standalone value that still reveals personality to close friends
Groups want play; pairs want depthGame and event modes for circles, with circle-specific prompts
Buy-in depends on everyone answering the same thingFeed lock: you can’t see anyone’s answers until you post your own — preserving low effort while guaranteeing reciprocity
Prompts drove off-platform conversation even when retention failedShareable prompt cards and quick share, so the conversation a prompt starts can travel

Note on Visual Direction: I argued for a restrained base palette — black, white, and a single accent — because users fill this product with images, video, and drawings. The interface had to recede so user content could carry the visual weight.

06 — Research → Go-to-Market

The concept traveled even when the app didn’t. The campaign was built on that.

The beta’s most useful signal was that people carried the prompts into conversations off-platform. I led the go-to-market translation on that basis: if the prompt is the thing that travels, put the prompt in the world and let the platform follow it.

  1. 24 prompts selected from ranked performance. Drawn from the highest-performing prompts across concept testing and the ranking study. Printed bare-bones on bright stock: one large question, the logo, a QR code, platform colors.
  2. Placement was a targeting decision, not a design one. Posters went up around graduate residences — people whose undergrad friends are now scattered — and around senior friend groups about to make the same transition. Both populations came directly out of needfinding.
  3. Posters went up after midnight, so the campaign appeared to arrive everywhere at once rather than accumulating.
  4. The QR skipped the download. Scanning let people answer the prompt and see how others answered without installing anything — a direct response to the cold-start finding. People could experience the core loop before committing to it.
  5. The launch event ran as a large group game mode, designed for mass onboarding in groups rather than individuals, addressing the network-benefit problem head-on.

I also built the brand direction — young, bold, in-your-face — into a compilation showing what Limbo looks like as a physical experience rather than a digital one: subway placements, logo patches, a pop-up phone booth.

07 — What I Know, and What I Don’t

What I Know, and What I Don’t

What the research established

Intimacy in digital spaces depends on contrast — a private room inside a public building, not a private building. Personal prompts outperform debate, opinion, and hypothetical prompts on both willingness to answer and interest in others’ answers, consistently across age segments. And the visibility of a platform suppresses candor independently of how much a user trusts the audience.

What it did not establish

The samples were small and drawn largely from convenience channels, with a skew toward women — 18 of 22 in the ranking study — and toward college students in the survey. The ranking finding is directional, not representative.

The beta ran on a platform without push notifications, so it is not a clean test of the mechanic. Seven days was also too short a window to answer the question I actually cared about, which was day-8 return. And nearly everything here is self-report.

What I would do differently

  • Gate the retention study on push notifications existing, or run a matched arm with manual daily SMS to isolate the variable properly.
  • Extend to a 14-day wave with a 30-day passive follow-up.
  • Replace the daily diary form. A daily form due before a moving deadline was too heavy an instrument for a product whose entire premise is low effort — the study’s compliance problem was a version of the product’s retention problem, and I should have seen that coming. In-app micro-prompts would have collected the same signal without the tax.
  • Instrument behaviorally. Time-to-response, skip rate by prompt category, and off-platform share events would have told me on day two what the diary took a week to not tell me.

What I’d measure next

  • Return rate on day 8 and day 30 with notifications live
  • Skip rate by prompt category
  • Time from prompt drop to response
  • Off-platform share events per prompt
  • Whether feed lock raises response rate or suppresses it
  • Whether standalone features (Scrapbook, Lightning Round) retain solo users past the cold-start window

Final Direction

Final Direction

Limbo shipped to the App Store as a daily prompt platform built on a small private core inside a larger public ecosystem — one-on-one depth in the center, play at the edges, and a prompt bank engineered so that being asked feels like being known rather than being screened.

I was the only researcher on a three-person team shipping under a fixed timeline. Every study had to earn its place against build time, which meant the work was as much about prioritization and research ops as it was about method. And when the evidence contradicted what the team believed — about debate prompts, about Circles, about our own retention hopes — my job was to say so early enough that it changed the build instead of the postmortem.

The part I’d point to: this went from a concept to a live product with a research trail behind every major feature decision. And the study that mattered most was the one that failed.
