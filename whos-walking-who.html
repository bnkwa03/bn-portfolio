Who's Walking Who?

Ethnographic Study: The Emotional & Structural Realities of Professional Dog Walkers

Role & Method

Role & Scope
Lead UX Researcher | Executed 20 individual hours of fieldwork & analysis (Partnered with Amelie O., Guided by Will O.)
Method & Dataset
Contextual Inquiry & Semi-Structured Interviews | ~40 total hours of field data across active walk routes, pickup/drop-off transit, and dog parks.

The Research Constraint

Sourcing an Unlisted Population

Professional, independent dog walkers are an unlisted, highly informal population that cannot be recruited through standard research panels or online screeners.

Execution

Discovered the underlying boarding-company and referral structures through initial fieldwork rather than desk research. Recruited participants entirely through live field intercepts during active work hours across Bay Area parks and walking routes.

The Core Spine

Reversing Stated Priors: Hypothesis vs. Reality

Field evidence directly disproved my initial research assumptions. Each reversal moved the study from a logistical framing toward the emotional and structural truth of the work.

Assumption 1

The primary challenge is physical/logistical (managing multiple dogs simultaneously).

Field Reality

The true strain is emotional—walkers act as an invisible support system absorbing the stress of Bay Area hustle culture so owners can focus on high-demand careers.

Assumption 2

People enter dog walking strictly out of love for animals.

Field Reality

Walkers light up around "difficult" or reactive dogs because guiding transformation serves as a quiet rehearsal for their own healing, dignity, and self-worth.

Assumption 3

The market is driven and organized by traditional gig-app platforms.

Field Reality

Walkers actively resist pure transactional app metrics, dealing with severe compassion fatigue, lack of healthcare/insurance infrastructure, and a desire for independent practice dignity.

Synthesis Process

Timeboxed Synthesis & Responsible AI Scoping

Timebox Constraint: Compressed ~40 hours of qualitative field data into defensible findings within a strict 3-day synthesis window.

Scoped AI Integration

Utilized AI strictly as a "third analyst" to help cluster raw observational notes, map transcript fragments, and surface evidence groupings. AI was explicitly restricted from making thematic decisions or writing final findings—all insights were human-derived and cross-verified against field notes.

Synthesized Findings & 5-Whys Analysis

Finding 1 — Dog walkers proudly define themselves as the people who make it possible to "hustle" in a hustle culture.

"[People] focus more on the outside, like working, but we don't see what's most important in life, like being around family...but I also understand you have to take care of your family." — Roberto Smith, walker at Ry The Pet Guy, @robs_dogs_

Unpacking Hustle Tension

Why 1

Dog walkers see their work as giving owners the freedom to rest, focus, or work longer hours; they keep the dog's needs met so the owner's life can keep moving.

Why 2

Dog walkers and owners both treat dogs as "family," but they also share an unspoken agreement that love can be outsourced if care is consistent.

Why 3

By providing that consistency, dog walkers begin to see themselves less as service workers and more as co-parents, invisible partners maintaining the family's emotional stability.

Why 4

That sense of quiet partnership gives them dignity and purpose: they keep others' lives humane in a culture that forgets how to slow down.

Why 5

The same culture that exhausts them depends on their calm; their labor sustains the very speed they're trying to escape.

★ Key Insight 1

Dog walkers are people who find dignity and identity in being the invisible support system that makes hustle culture livable, absorbing its emotional costs so others can keep moving, even as it quietly exhausts them.

Finding 2 — Dog walkers light up when describing the dogs that other people call "difficult."

"The dogs you get to work with...seeing them succeed...that's why you do it." — Fiona, owner of It's Not Magic Dog Training

Unpacking Favored Difficulty

Why 1

Dog walkers measure success in progress, so when a reactive or fearful dog begins to change, that progress is tangible proof of their care and skill.

Why 2

Anxious, scared, or frustrated dogs show their emotions openly, so their reactions make growth visible in a way that calm, obedient dogs don't.

Why 3

The honesty of emotive reaction resonates with dog walkers who have also felt misjudged or overlooked; they relate to dogs who are struggling to be understood.

Why 4

Dog walkers often see life as something that happens to them rather than for them: unstable housing, health issues, unfair employers, or failed systems. In caring for dogs who've also been neglected, reactive, or misunderstood, they reclaim the compassion they once needed themselves.

Why 5

By caring for "broken" or "troubled" dogs, walkers practice the kind of patience, safety, and steadiness they wish the world had offered them. Each walk becomes a quiet rehearsal for healing, both the dog's and their own.

★ Key Insight 2

Dog walkers find meaning, identity, and self-worth through guiding visible transformation in others, especially those who are misunderstood, because it affirms both their skill and their own capacity for healing.

Finding 3 — Many dog walkers don't own dogs, and those who do describe caring for them as part of their work.

"I have to spend so much time with other people's dogs, so when I get home. I don't feel like doing anything with my dogs." — Steph Haddad, owner of Good Sit: Dog Training and Group Play Services

Unpacking Compassion Fatigue

Why 1

Dog walkers spend so much time caring for other people's pets that they feel they don't have the capacity to care for their own.

Why 2

The process of regularly keeping tabs on how a dog and their owner feel mentally, physically, and emotionally makes them susceptible to burnout and compassion fatigue.

Why 3

Because every walk requires emotional regulation (reading the dog's mood, the owner's expectations, the energy of the street), their own emotions are constantly deprioritized.

Why 4

Over time, suppressing their own emotions to manage others' leads them to associate attachment with exhaustion, and care starts to feel like something that drains them rather than fulfilling them.

Why 5

To protect that fragile sense of calm, they begin to reserve intimacy only for paid care, creating emotional boundaries in their personal lives that mirror their professional ones.

★ Key Insight 3

Professional dog walkers experience care as emotionally labor-intensive work that consumes their capacity for attachment, causing them to ration care in their personal lives and redefine intimacy as something that must be tightly controlled.

Strategic Takeaway & North Star

Trimmed Product Focus: Avoid speculative feature sets. Instead, evaluate future product decisions against a single North Star question:

★ North Star

"Does this tool make their work feel more like sustainable care, or more like exploited labor?"

Example Strategic Direction: Shift platform success metrics away from speed and booking volume toward expertise recognition (e.g., behavioral progress tracking) and structural support (e.g., independent business tools and transparent boundaries).
