Literature Review

Main insights
  • Environmental Triggers: Impulse spending is influenced by environmental cues (e.g., product proximity, sales notifications, or social media exposure, emotional cues (e.g., feelings of FOMO, boredom, and negative mood), psychological needs, and social needs (Thorndike, 1937; Wood & Neal, 2009; Rodrigues et al., 2021; Jafri et al., 2025; Nguyen, 2025)
  • Designed Spending Environments: Retail ecosystems are intentionally designed to encourage impulse purchases by reducing friction through features like discounts, social influence, and intentional refund policies. (Moser et al., 2019)
  • Effective Interventions: Research suggests a number of intervention approaches to curtailing impulsive spending behavior. This included proactively strategizing, encouraging reflection, imposing spending limits, and enforcing delays before purchase. (Moser et al. 2019; Peetz & Davydenko, 2021; Davydenko & Peetz, 2024)
  • Personalization: Identifying a user's emotional needs is critical for creating reflective interventions that address the root cause. (Thorndike, 1937; Peetz & Davydenko, 2021)

Comparative Analysis

Conducted a comparative analysis of financial tools to understand the different approaches to financial management that currently exist in the market.

Key insights
  • Preventative tools, such as structured budgeting systems, often require high user effort, which can limit sustained use.
  • Several applications rely on cashback or reward incentives, which may unintentionally encourage spending rather than reduce it.
  • Most applications tend to focus on the amount spent, but not what is being purchased and why
  • Most applications did not challenge justifications made in the moment
OpportunityThere's a gap in the market for a solution that combines quantitative data on spending with qualitative insights on how a user feels in the moment of a purchase.

Baseline Study

Pre/post-study interview, diary study

9participants
5days logging

Ran research operations: wrote study outline, screener questions

Conducted a 5 day diary study with 9 participants who self-identified as convenience spenders with a desire to spend less impulsively to establish current impulse spending behaviors and gather feelings during the moment of an impulsive/convenient unplanned purchase. Participants filled out a brief questionnaire after each purchase.

Justification

This was the appropriate method because the goal was to understand the context surrounding purchases to better understand the qualitative aspects of spending that was deemed planned or unplanned by the spender. The brief questionnaire + pre/post study interviews also gave us both quantitative data (e.g., spending frequency, cost ranges, impulsivity and guilt ratings) and qualitative data (e.g., motivations, emotional responses, environmental context), providing a more holistic view of the behavior.

Synthesized through thematic analysis for pre, during, and post-interview raw data.

Main insights
  • Situational triggers that cause impulse spending were quickly reframed as "necessary", "productive", or "goal-aligned" to avoid guilt and financial stress.
  • Participants were not aware of the true extent of their spending until they logged purchases.
  • To participants, whether spending or unplanned purchases is seen as "problematic" is more so determined by how much the justification aligns with their identity and values than by impulsivity, cost, or frequency.Physical and temporal constraints transform discretionary spending into non-negotiable needs, shifting guilt from the purchase to the circumstances.
Main changes as a result of what we learnedA solution for impulse spending financial management platforms needs to illuminate the true frequency of and deeper justifications made for impulse purchases in a way that prevents spenders from utilizing physiological, social, temporal, or situational contexts to rationalize unplanned purchases.

Intervention Study

Pre/post-study interview, diary study with AI reflection agent I created

7participants (1 returning)
5days

Ran research ops, goal was to test whether setting daily spending intentions with AI-guided reflection on unplanned purchases increases awareness of impulse triggers and consequently reduces rapid post-purchase justification.

Created AI agent through custom GPT explicitly instructed to act as a reflective agent to get to the meaning behind immediate unplanned purchase justification.

Participants logged purchases they planned to make in the morning then reflected with agent at the end of the day, logging purchases they didn't log in the morning.

Main changes as a result of the study
  • Success in the platform needs to be about creating consistent awareness of impulses more than reducing the quantitative value of money spent
  • Morning planning was often more effective in creating awareness and avoiding small, discretionary purchases in the moment of impulse spending than the combined evening reflectionInsight-setting needs to be a central design pillar rather than one of two equally important steps in the platform.
  • AI tone and persistence critically shaped how a participant engaged. If the AI felt too pushy or invasive, participants felt shamed or manipulated.The AI coach's tone and depth in the platform needs to be lighter for low-stakes purchases and proportional for emotionally significant ones, with tighter guardrails to prevent shame or over-validation.

Assumption Test

14respondents
5strong desire to stop

Rapid testing to support the primary assumption surrounding our platform's main intervention mechanism: Impulse spenders want to stop spending impulsively.

Result

Only 5 of 14 self-identified impulse spenders expressed a strong desire to stop spending impulsively. Though n=14 is a small sample size, the results should have provided a strong enough signal to show that the majority in this group would reaffirm our assumption, even in a small representation.
