Limbo

Can a daily question keep distant friends close?

Sole UX Researcher · 10 Weeks · 2 Design Engineers · Live on the App Store

I led mixed-methods research from early needfinding through a live TestFlight alpha, where a 0% seven-day retention rate revealed that we were onboarding a social product like an individual product.

01 — The Research

What replaces the spontaneous interaction friends lose after graduation?

After college, friends scatter across cities and lose the forced proximity that kept them close. Reaching out starts to feel like it needs a reason. I ran research in four moves — from understanding the problem to watching real behavior on a live build:

11

Interviews

Understand the friction that stalls post-grad friendships.

41

Prompt studies

Test what actually makes people want to respond.

20

Live alpha

Observe real behavior on a TestFlight build over a week.

→

Follow-up

Diagnose why retention collapsed, and what to change.

02 — Findings

Finding 1 — People wanted permission to talk about nothing important.

New grads told me catch-up calls felt too heavy — reserved for when you’d “accumulated a list” of big updates. What they actually wanted was a low-stakes reason to reach out about nothing in particular.

“When you don’t see a person that often, you feel like the time you have together just has to be spent catching up.” — Skye, study participant

So we built daily prompts that expire after 24 hours: a fresh, low-effort excuse to send a quick reply without needing a big update. A follow-up prompt-ranking study (41 participants) showed people preferred specific, playful questions over generic check-ins — so I built a rotating prompt library across playful, hypothetical, and reflective formats.

01

Playful

“What random hobby have you always thought about trying?”

02

Hypothetical

“If you woke up in someone else’s body, how would you convince your loved ones?”

03

Reflective

“What are you still lowkey scared of?”

Finding 2 — The prompts weren’t enough if your friends weren’t there.

We shipped a live iOS alpha to 20 users. The prompts landed — but the product didn’t hold anyone.

55%

answered at least one prompt

0%

maintained a seven-day streak

What we thought: prompt quality would drive engagement.

What we learned: seeing friends’ answers drove people to return more than posting their own (5.1/10 vs. 4.3/10). Users who joined without their friends hit an empty feed and stopped within 1–2 days.

What changed: we stopped onboarding individuals and began onboarding intact friend groups.

03 — The Pivot

Research changed our unit of onboarding.

—

Before

A person downloads Limbo → enters an empty feed → waits for friends to show up.

→

After

A friend group joins together → enters a populated circle → immediately sees responses.

I brought the alpha findings to the team and advocated for shifting acquisition from individual users to pre-formed groups of 3–15 friends.

Reflection

What I’d do differently.

The biggest mistake in our alpha wasn’t the interface — it was the study design. We recruited individuals to evaluate a product whose value depended on groups. If I ran this again, I’d recruit intact friend groups from the start and measure engagement at both the individual and group level.

We also ran a “Limbo IRL” launch event — physical prompt cards and QR onboarding to seed group context before anyone downloaded the app. [Artifact: Limbo IRL event photo]
